# Mission practice and its sources

Habla mission practice is an independent adaptation of principles described in public CIA language-training sources. It is not an official CIA course, an intelligence qualification or a reproduction of classified training. The cited descriptions do not establish a guaranteed learning speed or validate this app's effects.

## Public evidence and attribution

The CIA's [2022 description of the Intelligence Language Institute](https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/language-empowers-mission/) describes instruction and testing tailored to mission needs, reading, speaking, listening, cultural knowledge, and tailored phrases and idioms. It also describes interactive computer-assisted instruction. This supports purposeful contextual practice as a design direction; it does not supply a complete public curriculum or establish a uniquely superior method.

The historical [Language Highlights, January 1972](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP86-00244R000200300011-0.pdf), published by the CIA Office of Training Language School, describes everyday target-language immersion after learners acquired foundations (printed pages 1–4). It questions instant-fluency advertising and very intense immersion for rank beginners. Habla therefore offers preparation and an optional Spanish-first rehearsal, with help and breaks available.

The same publication describes an experimental adoption of **FSI-developed Programmatic Spanish** (printed pages 15–16), including self-paced material, self-tests, audio study, instructor checks and more conversation as foundations develop. This program was developed by the Foreign Service Institute, not invented by CIA. Habla's staged sequence is a modern product adaptation. It does not reproduce that course or its historical workload and outcome claims.

The publication's discussion of proficiency testing (printed pages 9–13) separates language accuracy and skill areas from confidence and job success. Habla reports actual attempts and requested support. It does not award CIA, ILR or CEFR proficiency. A1–B1 labels continue to describe the authored material.

[CIA officer accounts from 2017](https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/using-foreign-languages-at-cia/) describe immersion preparation and practical language use, including Spanish in Latin America. These accounts support connecting study to everyday application, but are descriptive accounts rather than controlled learning research.

Sources checked on 24 September 2026 with Firecrawl. Contemporary descriptions and historical material are identified separately. The app's existing retrieval, spacing and feedback design continues to be grounded in the separate [learning research review](research.md).

## Implemented sequence

1. **Brief:** choose one of the 24 units and read its concrete communication goal.
2. **Prepare:** read explanations, notice formality and useful patterns, and listen to authored model phrases with browser speech.
3. **Rehearse:** take every role in the authored dialogue. The current reply and future dialogue replies stay hidden until requested or submitted. Optional Spanish-first mode hides the English meaning too. Showing English or a model is retained as support evidence.
4. **Adapt:** respond to each unit's existing changed-context task. English task instructions identify what to communicate; model answers remain hidden until requested or submitted.
5. **Debrief:** view attempted replies, model matches without requested help and use of support. Record an optional real-world task check-in and a short reflection.

The flow is self-paced, can pause and resume, and saves drafts, support, position, attempts and check-in through the parent application's state. It reuses existing content and requires no paid model or infrastructure.

## Interpretation and limits

- Model matching accepts curated alternatives. A different response can be valid even if it does not match. Learners can compare the model and continue.
- A model match after preparation is immediate practice, not proof of lasting retention. Mission practice does not advance spaced-review mastery.
- Reading an instruction in English is distinguished from revealing an English model meaning during Spanish-first rehearsal. English task instructions remain available in the adaptation stage.
- The real-world check-in is a learner's self-report. It does not create graded evidence or certify an ability.
- Text input and browser speech do not measure pronunciation. Mission practice is separate from open-topic conversation coaching.
- Cultural/register notes are general and context-dependent. This feature does not claim the depth of instructor-led cultural training.
- Optional reflection stays in local progress and user-created backups. There are no prepopulated personal learner details.

## Validation

Pure progression tests cover all 24 units, explicit submission and continuation, model visibility in rendered markup, English/model support retention, pause and restore, changed-context grading, self-report separation and malformed or forged imports. The parent app must pass current units to `parseMissionSession` during import/reference validation so unknown units and impossible completion records are rejected.
